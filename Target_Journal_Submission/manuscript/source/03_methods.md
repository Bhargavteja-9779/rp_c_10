# Methods

## Study design and reporting
This was a retrospective development and multinational external-validation study using publicly available, de-identified ambulatory electrocardiogram (ECG) databases. Reporting follows TRIPOD+AI [@collins2024] and the 2025 EHRA/HRS/ESC e-Cardiology scientific statement on artificial intelligence in clinical electrophysiology [@ehra_ai2025]; the EHRA AI checklist is provided as Supplementary material. The analysis plan, including hyper-parameters, random seeds, endpoints and statistical tests, was written and version-controlled before any model was trained (Supplementary material S1).

## Data sources
The development pool comprised six PhysioNet databases recorded in the USA [@goldberger2000]: the Long-Term AF Database (LTAFDB) [@petrutiu2007], the MIT-BIH AF Database (AFDB) [@moody1983], the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database (MITDB) [@moody2001], the BIDMC Congestive Heart Failure Database (CHFDB) [@baim1986], and the MIT-BIH Normal Sinus Rhythm databases (NSRDB, NSR2DB). External validation used three cohorts that were never used for training, calibration or tuning: SHDB-AF, 24-h two-channel Holter recordings of Japanese patients with paroxysmal AF annotated beat-by-beat by a cardiologist [@tsutsui2025]; CPSC2021, dynamic Holter recordings from the 4th China Physiological Signal Challenge [@cpsc2021]; and Icentia11k, single-lead patch recordings from Canadian outpatients with technologist-reviewed beat and rhythm labels [@icentia_physionet; @tan2019], from which {{D:-|icentia_sampled}} patients were sampled uniformly at random (fixed seed, without inspecting labels) and their first ten 70-min segments used. Only beat and rhythm annotation files were used (Table 1; versions, licences and exclusions in Supplementary S2).

## Reference standard and units of analysis
RR intervals were derived from the database beat annotations (automatic R-peak annotations for AFDB and SHDB-AF, reviewed annotations elsewhere); intervals outside 0.2–3.0 s or outside labelled rhythm segments were excluded. Each RR interval inherited the reference rhythm at its terminating beat. RR series were cut into non-overlapping windows of 60 consecutive valid intervals. A window was labelled AF if ≥50% of its duration was annotated AF. The reference AF burden of a patient was the AF-annotated time divided by the analysed time (the total duration of complete windows; trailing runs of fewer than 60 valid intervals were excluded from both reference and estimates); atrial flutter was counted as non-AF (sensitivity analysis: AF+flutter). Recordings of the same patient were aggregated, so the unit of analysis was the patient.

## Window-level detectors
Because the proposed method operates on any detector's output, three detectors spanning model complexity were trained: (i) Classical-LR, a logistic regression on three classical irregularity features (normalised RMSSD, Shannon entropy, turning-point ratio) [@dash2009]; (ii) LightGBM, gradient-boosted trees on 16 RR features including sample-entropy-based irregularity (pre-specified primary detector); and (iii) RRNet, a convolutional–bidirectional recurrent network on the normalised RR sequence, re-implemented in the spirit of ArNet/ArNet2 [@chocron2021; @biton2023] (architecture in Supplementary material). Each detector outputs a window probability *p*.

## Burden estimators
For patient *i* with windows *j* of duration *d*<sub>ij</sub> the following estimators were compared, all calibrated on held-out development patients only: classify-and-count (CC, threshold 0.5); CC after a three-window median filter (CC-MF); probabilistic CC (PCC, mean probability) [@bella2010] and PCC after source isotonic recalibration (PCC-iso, the label-free analogue of probability recalibration [@haekal2026]); adjusted CC (ACC), which corrects CC for the source true- and false-positive rates [@forman2008]; probabilistic ACC (PACC) [@bella2010]; expectation–maximisation prior adjustment (EMQ) [@saerens2002]; and a two-state hidden Markov model with fixed source rhythm dynamics (HMM-src) [@rabiner1989].

**Proposed estimator (PA-HMMQ).** Each patient's window sequence is modelled as a two-state (non-AF/AF) hidden Markov chain. Emission likelihoods are a function of the detector output only and are held fixed (label-shift assumption: the appearance of an AF window does not depend on how much AF the patient has). The rhythm dynamics—initial distribution π<sub>i</sub> and transition matrix A<sub>i</sub>—are re-estimated for every patient by maximum-a-posteriori expectation–maximisation with a Dirichlet prior centred on the source dynamics A<sub>src</sub> estimated on calibration patients:

A<sub>i</sub>(k,l) ∝ Σ<sub>t</sub> ξ<sub>it</sub>(k,l) + κ A<sub>src</sub>(k,l),

where ξ are the posterior expected transitions and κ (pre-specified 10) the prior strength. Burden is the duration-weighted posterior probability of AF, B̂<sub>i</sub> = Σ<sub>j</sub> d<sub>ij</sub> γ<sub>ij</sub> / Σ<sub>j</sub> d<sub>ij</sub>, γ<sub>ij</sub> = P(AF<sub>ij</sub> | all windows of patient *i*). This generalises EMQ (κ→0, no temporal dependence) and HMM-src (κ→∞). The pre-registered version used emission likelihoods estimated as class-conditional histograms of logit(*p*) on calibration patients; a variant using the detector probability directly as likelihood (e(AF) ∝ *p*, e(non-AF) ∝ 1−*p*) was pre-specified as an ablation. Following a decision rule recorded after a one-seed pipeline check and before the full analysis, and evaluated on development patients only (Supplementary S1, Amendment A1; the pipeline check had also displayed errors for 40-patient subsets of two external cohorts), the variant with the lower development error was designated the final method; both are reported throughout. To isolate the contribution of per-patient adaptation, every PA-HMMQ variant was also compared with the HMM using identical emissions but fixed source dynamics (post hoc). A further post-hoc revision (R1) selected the emission sharpness separately for each detector on calibration patients (likelihood ratio (*p*/(1−*p*))<sup>1/T</sup>, T ∈ {0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8}, or the histogram model).

**Conformal burden intervals.** Posterior burden samples were drawn by forward-filtering backward-sampling (200 samples) [@carter1994], giving a model-based standard deviation σ̂<sub>i</sub>. On a separate set of calibration patients, nonconformity scores r = |B − B̂|/(σ̂ + ε) (ε = 0.002) were computed, and the ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉-th smallest score q̂ defined the interval B̂<sub>i</sub> ± q̂(σ̂<sub>i</sub> + ε), clipped to [0,1], with α = 0.10. Under exchangeability of patients, this guarantees ≥90% marginal coverage [@vovk2005; @lei2018]; normalising the score by the model-based posterior standard deviation makes interval width adapt to each patient's rhythm pattern. Constant-width conformal intervals around CC, PCC-iso and PA-HMMQ, and the uncalibrated posterior credible interval, served as comparators.

## Data partitioning and leakage control
For each of five pre-specified seeds, development patients were split, stratified by database and AF presence, into training (45%; 15% of these for early stopping), calibration A (15%; all source calibration quantities), calibration B (20%; conformal quantile only) and test (20%) sets. No patient appeared in more than one set. All estimators used the same detector outputs and the same calibration-A patients, thresholds were fixed at 0.5, and no estimator was tuned on test or external data. Because Amendment A1 used calibration-B and development test patients to choose the final emission model, development test results of the final variant are not independent of its selection; the external cohorts are the only fully independent test. An automated audit fingerprinting all RR windows and recordings across all databases identified one exact duplicate recording in SHDB-AF (two files of the same subject with identical RR series), which was removed (Supplementary S2). Features and RR normalisation were computed within each window only.

## Endpoints and statistical analysis
The pre-specified primary endpoint was the per-patient absolute burden error (percentage points, pp) in the pooled external cohorts with the LightGBM detector; because Icentia11k contributes most pooled patients (largely AF-free), per-cohort results are reported alongside. Per-patient errors were averaged over the five seeds and compared between the proposed estimator and each comparator using two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm correction across the eight comparators [@wilcoxon1945; @holm1979]; effect sizes are the mean and median paired differences with patient-level bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (2000 resamples) and matched-pairs rank-biserial correlations [@kerby2014]. Secondary endpoints were Bland–Altman bias and limits of agreement [@blandaltman1986], Lin's concordance correlation coefficient [@lin1989], false burden in AF-free patients, classification of patients by burden thresholds (≥30 s of AF, ≥1%, ≥10%; exact McNemar tests on seed-majority classifications), conformal coverage (Clopper–Pearson 95% CI) and interval width, and window-level discrimination and calibration of the detectors. Only the comparison of the final PA-HMMQ with the eight comparators (LightGBM, pooled external cohorts) is regarded as confirmatory; all other tests are exploratory and not corrected across families. Because many AF-free patients receive exactly zero burden from counting-type estimators but small non-zero posterior burdens from HMM estimators, rank-based tests partly reflect differences far below clinical relevance; mean paired differences with bootstrap CIs were therefore used as the primary effect size for interpretation, and a post-hoc sensitivity analysis repeated the tests with estimates reported at 0.1-percentage-point resolution. Bootstrap CIs do not include between-seed model variability (reported separately as SDs), and Clopper–Pearson intervals were computed from seed-averaged coverage. Summary statistics are mean ± standard deviation over seeds. Analyses not in the pre-registered plan (fair-comparator tests, emission selection R1, exclusion of flutter patients) are labelled post hoc. Robustness was assessed by re-analysing SHDB-AF after simulated missed R peaks (2%, 5%), false R peaks (2%, 5%), R-peak timing jitter (10, 25 ms) and premature beats with compensatory pauses (5%, 10%). Sensitivity analyses varied κ, the training-set size (25%, 50%) and the window length (30, 120 beats). Analyses used Python 3.13 with PyTorch, scikit-learn, LightGBM and SciPy; all code and the commands regenerating every number, table and figure are publicly available (Data availability).

## Ethics
Only publicly available, de-identified databases were used, under their licences (ODC-By 1.0; CPSC2021 CC BY 4.0; Icentia11k CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, non-commercial research use); the original studies obtained ethical approval and consent as described in the cited publications. No new data were collected, so additional ethical approval and consent were not required.
