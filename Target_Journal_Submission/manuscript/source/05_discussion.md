# Discussion

In {{D:ext_total|patients}} patients from three continents who contributed {{D:ext_total|hours}} hours of ambulatory recordings, aggregating detector outputs with a hidden Markov burden layer reduced the mean per-patient AF-burden error compared with counting AF-classified windows and with eight alternative estimators, for three detectors of very different complexity, and markedly reduced false AF diagnoses in AF-free patients; conformal calibration produced per-patient intervals whose coverage met the 90% target in two of three external cohorts but fell short in the Japanese cohort. Equally important are the negative findings: the pre-registered specification did not outperform counting, per-patient re-estimation of rhythm dynamics—the component we expected to correct patient-specific prevalence—added nothing, and the absolute improvement was modest.

## Interpretation
Burden is a property of a patient's rhythm trajectory: an isolated AF-like window inside long sinus rhythm, or a short regular stretch inside persistent AF, is more likely a classification error than a rhythm change. A temporal model removes much of this window-level noise, which is why median filtering and hidden Markov smoothing improved on counting. The magnitude of the gain depended on how sharply detector outputs were translated into evidence: calibration-set class-conditional histograms flattened the likelihood ratios because window labels near rhythm transitions are noisy, letting sticky source dynamics over-smooth genuine rhythm changes, whereas the detector's own probability preserved discriminating evidence. Contrary to our hypothesis, patient-specific prevalence did not need explicit correction once temporal structure was modelled—with sharp emissions the likelihood dominates the prior over rhythm dynamics, which explains the insensitivity to κ. Quantification estimators designed for independent samples (ACC, PACC, EMQ) [@forman2008; @saerens2002; @bella2010] did not reliably improve on counting, and recalibrating window probabilities on the development population made burden estimates worse. A probability calibrated on a development population embeds that population's AF prevalence and pulls every patient's estimate towards it; good classifier calibration is therefore not sufficient for unbiased burden estimation, which complements the observation that recalibration with labelled target data improves probability-based burden [@haekal2026].

## Clinical implications
AF burden is increasingly used to stratify risk and to evaluate therapy [@chen2018; @go2018; @becher2024], and device- or patch-derived burden informs decisions about subclinical AF [@kirchhof2023; @healey2024]. The clinically most visible effect was the reduction of spurious AF in AF-free patients: with counting, {{fpr:LightGBM|CC|ext_pooled|present_30s}} of external patients without ≥30 s of reference AF were assigned ≥30 s of AF (final PA-HMMQ {{fpr:LightGBM|PA-HMMQ[rawE]|ext_pooled|present_30s}}), which in long-term monitoring may trigger further investigation or anticoagulation discussions. This came at the cost of missing some patients with very little AF, a trade-off that should be weighed against the purpose of monitoring. The burden layer requires no retraining of an existing detector, adds well under a second of computation per 24-h recording, and supplies an interval indicating whether a reported burden is distinguishable from zero or from a decision threshold. Because conformal coverage is guaranteed only for patients exchangeable with the calibration set, intervals should be recalibrated locally when a device or population differs substantially, as recently argued for AI-ECG classifiers [@abbasi2026].

## Comparison with previous work
Deep RR-interval models such as ArNet and ArNet2 [@chocron2021; @biton2023] and feature-based pipelines [@karimizadeh2026] report strong window-level performance and low burden error, and commercial AI burden agrees closely with manual assessment in selected cohorts [@hennings2023]. Those studies report point estimates; the present work is complementary, operating on top of any detector, comparing aggregation strategies systematically and adding patient-level uncertainty. Consistent with ArNet2, atrial flutter was the dominant failure mode of RR-based burden estimation [@biton2023]: flutter with variable conduction produces irregular RR intervals indistinguishable from AF without atrial activity information. Whether flutter time should count towards burden is partly definitional—stroke-prevention recommendations for flutter largely follow those for AF [@vangelder2024]—and with an AF+flutter reference the errors of all estimators fell substantially.

## Limitations
First, the pre-registered specification failed; the final emission model was chosen on development data after a one-seed check (Amendment A1) and the per-detector emission selection (R1) and flutter-exclusion analyses were post hoc, so the external results for the final method, although never used for any decision, should be regarded as requiring confirmation in further cohorts. Second, the improvement was modest (a mean of {{test:LightGBM|PA-HMMQ[rawE]|ext_pooled|CC|mean_diff}} pp versus counting), rank-based tests were not significant against three comparators, and the development test set did not favour the final over the pre-registered variant. Third, the study used retrospective public databases with heterogeneous annotation processes; reference labels, particularly in Icentia11k, may contain errors, and R peaks were taken from database annotations rather than from a uniform detector, although simulated R-peak errors were examined. Fourth, the development pool was modest ({{D:dev_total|patients}} patients) and from one country. Fifth, conformal coverage is marginal over patients, was lower in patients with AF, and is not guaranteed under distribution shift. Sixth, 60-beat windows limit resolution for episodes shorter than about 30–60 s. Seventh, Icentia11k was represented by a random subset of patients and segments, and the reduced-training and window-length analyses omitted it. Finally, no outcome data were available, and photoplethysmography-based wearables, which are increasingly used for burden monitoring [@smartwatch_europace2026], were not studied.
