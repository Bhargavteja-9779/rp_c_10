# Discussion

This study reframed automated AF-burden estimation as a per-patient quantification problem and showed, in {{D:ext_total|patients}} patients from three continents who contributed {{D:ext_total|hours}} hours of ambulatory recordings, that a patient-adaptive hidden Markov quantifier reduced burden error relative to classify-and-count and eight established alternatives, consistently across three detectors of very different complexity, and that conformal calibration turned its posterior uncertainty into intervals with close to nominal coverage outside the development population.

## Interpretation
Two mechanisms explain the gain. First, burden is a property of a patient's rhythm *trajectory*: an isolated AF-like window inside long sinus rhythm, or a short regular stretch inside persistent AF, is more likely a classification error than a rhythm change. A temporal model therefore removes much of the window-level noise that classify-and-count propagates into burden, which is why median filtering and the fixed-dynamics HMM already improved on counting. Second, rhythm dynamics differ between patients—from AF-free to paroxysmal to persistent—and the source dynamics learned from a development population act as a prior that is wrong for many individual patients; re-estimating them per patient is the temporal analogue of prior-shift correction in quantification [@saerens2002; @forman2008]. The ablation indicates that, in these data, most of the improvement came from the temporal model with sharp emission likelihoods, while per-patient adaptation contributed a smaller, consistent increment.

Two negative findings are informative. Recalibrating window probabilities on source patients (PCC-iso) made burden estimates *worse* in every cohort. A probability calibrated on a development population embeds that population's AF prevalence and pulls every patient's estimate towards it; good classifier calibration is therefore not sufficient for unbiased prevalence estimation, consistent with quantification theory [@bella2010; @esuli2023] and complementary to the observation that target-cohort recalibration with labelled data restores probability-based burden [@haekal2026]. Likewise, the pre-registered emission model—class-conditional histograms on calibration patients—flattened the likelihood ratios because window labels near rhythm transitions are noisy, letting sticky source dynamics over-smooth genuine rhythm changes; this is why the simpler probability-emission variant performed better, a choice made on development data only.

## Clinical implications
AF burden is increasingly used to stratify risk and to evaluate therapy [@chen2018; @go2018; @becher2024], and device- or patch-derived burden informs decisions about subclinical AF [@kirchhof2023; @healey2024]. An absolute error of a few percentage points matters most near decision-relevant thresholds and in patients with low burden, where false-positive windows from ectopy can create apparent AF. The proposed layer requires no retraining of an existing detector, adds milliseconds of computation per 24-h recording, and supplies an interval that tells the reader whether a reported burden of, for example, 2% is distinguishable from zero or from 10%. Because coverage is guaranteed only for patients exchangeable with the calibration set, the intervals should be recalibrated locally when a device or population differs substantially, as recently argued for AI-ECG classifiers [@abbasi2026].

## Comparison with previous work
Deep RR-interval models such as ArNet and ArNet2 [@chocron2021; @biton2023] and feature-based pipelines [@karimizadeh2026] reported strong window-level performance and low burden error, and commercial AI burden agrees closely with manual assessment in selected cohorts [@hennings2023]. Those studies report point estimates only; the present work is complementary, operating on top of any such detector, and adds bias correction and patient-level uncertainty. Consistent with ArNet2, atrial flutter was the dominant failure mode of RR-based burden estimation [@biton2023]: flutter with variable conduction produces irregular RR intervals indistinguishable from AF without atrial activity information. Whether flutter time should count towards burden is partly definitional—stroke prevention recommendations for flutter largely follow those for AF [@vangelder2024]—and with an AF+flutter reference the errors of all estimators fell substantially.

## Limitations
First, the study used retrospective public databases with heterogeneous annotation processes; reference labels, particularly in the large Icentia11k cohort, may contain errors, and R peaks were taken from database annotations rather than from a uniform detector, although simulated R-peak errors were examined. Second, the development pool was modest ({{D:dev_total|patients}} patients) and from one country, although this makes the external results more conservative. Third, the absolute improvement was modest (a mean of {{test:LightGBM|PA-HMMQ[rawE]|ext_pooled|CC|mean_diff}} pp versus classify-and-count), the per-patient median difference was near zero because most external patients were AF-free, and the benefit was concentrated in a minority of patients; the clinical value of this improvement for decisions was not tested. Fourth, the emission model of the final method was changed from the pre-registered specification on the basis of development data (Amendment A1); both versions are reported. Fifth, conformal coverage is marginal over patients and was lower in patients with AF than in AF-free patients; it is not guaranteed under distribution shift. Sixth, 60-beat windows limit resolution for episodes shorter than about 30–60 s. Seventh, Icentia11k was represented by a random subset of patients and segments, and the reduced-training and window-length analyses omitted Icentia11k for computational reasons. Finally, no outcome data were available, and photoplethysmography-based wearables, which are increasingly used for burden monitoring [@smartwatch_europace2026], were not studied.
