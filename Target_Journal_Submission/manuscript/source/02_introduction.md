# Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is increasingly characterised by how much of the time a patient spends in the arrhythmia rather than by its mere presence. AF burden—the proportion of monitored time in AF—is associated with ischaemic stroke and other outcomes, is an endpoint of rhythm-control trials, and has become central to the interpretation of device-detected subclinical AF [@chen2018; @go2018; @becher2024; @kirchhof2023; @healey2024]. The 2024 European Society of Cardiology guidelines acknowledge burden as a clinically relevant descriptor while noting that its measurement is method-dependent [@vangelder2024]. With the spread of long-term Holter, patch, wearable and implantable monitoring, burden is now almost always computed automatically.

Automated burden estimation is usually performed by running a window-level AF detector over the recording and counting the windows classified as AF. Beat-to-beat (RR) interval detectors range from classical irregularity statistics [@tateno2001; @dash2009; @lake2011; @petrenas2015] and feature-based machine learning [@kennedy2016; @karimizadeh2026] to deep recurrent networks evaluated across geographies [@faust2018; @andersen2019; @chocron2021; @biton2023]. These studies report excellent window-level discrimination, but burden is a patient-level *proportion*, and counting classified windows is a biased estimator of a proportion whenever sensitivity and specificity are imperfect: false-positive windows inflate burden in patients with little AF, false negatives deflate it in patients with persistent AF, and the size of both errors changes with the patient's own AF prevalence [@forman2008]. Recent work has shown that burden derived from averaged detector probabilities degrades under external dataset shift unless probabilities are recalibrated with labelled data from the target cohort [@haekal2026]. In addition, current tools report burden as a single number without an uncertainty statement, although a clinician deciding whether a low burden is real needs to know how wrong the estimate could plausibly be.

The machine-learning field of *quantification* addresses exactly the problem of estimating class proportions in a new sample whose prevalence differs from that of the training data [@forman2008; @saerens2002; @bella2010; @gonzalez2017; @esuli2023], and conformal prediction provides distribution-free uncertainty intervals with finite-sample coverage guarantees [@vovk2005; @lei2018; @angelopoulos2023]. Neither has, to our knowledge, been applied to AF burden: conformal methods in electrocardiography have so far targeted diagnostic classification [@abbasi2026; @elallam2026], and quantification estimators assume independent samples, whereas rhythm windows are strongly dependent in time.

We therefore treated AF-burden estimation as a per-patient quantification problem and developed a detector-agnostic burden layer—a hidden Markov model quantifier that models the temporal persistence of rhythm and can re-estimate each patient's rhythm dynamics (patient-adaptive hidden Markov quantifier, PA-HMMQ)—with distribution-free conformal burden intervals. Following a pre-registered plan, we tested in external cohorts from Japan, China and Canada (i) whether it reduces per-patient burden error compared with classify-and-count and established quantification and smoothing methods, (ii) which of its components are responsible, (iii) whether its intervals attain nominal coverage, and (iv) how robust it is to R-peak errors, ectopy and reduced training data.
